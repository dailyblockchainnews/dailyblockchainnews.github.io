OKX urges real-time alerts after $6.3 billion Binance hack

After Binance’s $6.3 billion breach, Coinbase adjusted offline key systems and OKX’s Star Xu urged exchanges to share alerts and coordinate freezes.

The Hashbeam Desk·Nov 3, 2026 · 06:46 UTC·3 min read

Crypto exchanges are tightening defenses and calling for a shared, real-time response to hacks after attackers stole an estimated $6.3 billion from Binance. Reuters’ report said Coinbase adjusted systems that store cryptocurrency keys offline, while OKX CEO Star Xu called for exchanges and other industry participants to share alerts and coordinate rapid responses, including temporary freezes of suspected stolen funds.

What happened inside Binance?

Binance halted withdrawals after the attack on Thursday, October 29, and said it would use company funds to cover the losses. Co-CEO Richard Teng said the attack may have begun with a compromised software engineer’s laptop, which gave attackers access to systems used to process withdrawals. Binance later found additional breach points, and software designed to erase traces of the attack complicated the investigation, Reuters reported.

Binance’s explanation remains preliminary: Teng described a possible starting point, not a completed account of the intrusion. Reuters reported that the bulk of the theft took about 20 minutes. U.S. blockchain analysis firms TRM Labs and Chainalysis said the activity resembled previous attacks attributed to North Korea; that attribution is an assessment by the firms, not a confirmed finding about the attackers’ identity.

What response are exchanges proposing?

Xu’s proposed mechanism is an industry-wide alert system. When a participant detects a hack, it would immediately share information with others, allowing them to coordinate a response and temporarily freeze funds suspected of being stolen. That proposal links incident detection to actions other firms or asset issuers can take while funds are still traceable and have not been moved through laundering routes.

Reuters reported that Coinbase made precautionary adjustments to its offline key-storage systems, while Kraken said it was monitoring incidents for lessons it could apply to its defenses. Kraken also said its full-time teams look for vulnerabilities every day. The report did not describe a shared alert system as already operating; Xu was calling for participants to develop one.

The distinction matters because the ability to stop transfers depends on the asset. Tether said it froze nearly $1.57 billion linked to the Binance attack, and Circle said it froze $800 million. Those issuer actions can restrict transfers of their stablecoins. Bitcoin and ether have no single controlling entity, so there is no central issuer that can freeze those coins. A rapid alert could help exchanges identify and block deposits of stolen assets on their own platforms, but it cannot give every token the same freeze mechanism.

Can the proposed system stop a theft in progress?

It could shorten the delay between detection and coordinated action, which is the window Xu’s proposal targets. The sequence would be: one participant detects suspicious activity, shares the alert, and other participants assess associated addresses and decide whether to block or freeze assets they control. The proposal does not establish how participants would verify alerts, what evidence would trigger a freeze, or how they would handle false positives.

Those limits are material in a cross-chain theft. A freeze by a stablecoin issuer affects the issuer’s token; it does not stop movement of bitcoin or ether, or compel every exchange to reject a deposit. Reuters reported that Chainalysis saw laundering patterns it had not encountered before. The exchanges’ call is therefore for faster coordination across existing controls, not a single technical switch that can reverse a blockchain transaction.
