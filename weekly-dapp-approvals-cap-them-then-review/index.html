Weekly dapp approvals: cap them, then review

Should you revoke approvals for dapps you use every week? Usually keep them capped: limit token exposure while avoiding repeated approval and revocation fees.

The Hashbeam Desk·Nov 24, 2026 · 06:38 UTC·5 min read

After a swap, keep a frequently used dapp’s approval only when you can cap the amount; that limits what its contract can spend without paying to revoke and approve again every week. For a wallet-by-wallet view of token permissions, revokecash adds a way to inspect and manage approvals. An approval is an ERC-20 token allowance for a specific spender contract, not simply a record that your wallet connected to a site.

How do the four approval choices compare?

The allowance sets the maximum amount a spender can move from your wallet. Under the ERC-20 standard, calling approve sets that allowance, and a later call can overwrite it. A spender uses transferFrom to move tokens within the approved limit. So an unlimited approval leaves the full token balance exposed while it stands; a capped allowance limits that exposure to its remaining amount.

  • Unlimited standing approval: Pay an approval fee once, then swap without a new approval each time. The trade-off is that the spender can move the full balance of that token at any time while the allowance remains. A bug or compromise in the spender contract can therefore put more at risk than the next swap requires.
  • Capped standing approval: Approve an amount that covers several planned swaps but is smaller than the balance you want to protect. You still pay an approval fee when setting or refreshing the cap, and the approved amount remains available to the spender until used or changed. This is often the practical middle ground for a known dapp used weekly.
  • Exact-amount approval per use: Approve only the amount for the next swap. When a standard allowance is fully consumed by that swap, its remaining allowance is zero; there is no separate revoke transaction to pay for. Each later swap needs a new approval, though, so this option trades less standing exposure for more approval transactions.
  • Approve then revoke: Set an allowance, make the swap, then send another transaction to set it back to zero. That is two fee-bearing transactions per cycle and, when the swap consumes the exact allowance, the same remaining allowance as exact-amount approval. Revoking is useful when an unwanted allowance is already standing; doing it after every exact-sized swap adds no allowance reduction.

The network changes the fee side of the choice. On Ethereum, where approval transactions can cost more, a capped standing allowance can save most of the repeated approval fees for routine swaps. On a cheaper L2 such as Arbitrum, exact-amount approvals may cost little enough to suit someone who wants no leftover allowance. The amount at risk and whether the spender contract can change its logic still matter on either chain.

Take a constructed example: a regular Uniswap user swaps the same token each week and keeps a larger balance of it in the wallet. An unlimited approval would leave that whole balance within the spender’s reach. A cap sized for several ordinary swaps could reduce the amount exposed while avoiding a new approval each week. If the user instead makes a one-off swap on Arbitrum, approving the exact amount can leave zero allowance after it is spent without an extra revoke fee.

Which setting fits your dapp?

Choose a capped standing approval when the dapp is well known and its spender contract is immutable. Choose exact amounts when the contract is upgradeable, or when the wallet holds a large balance of the approved token. An upgradeable contract can change its logic behind the same address, so seeing a familiar spender address does not by itself mean the code behind it is unchanged. A large balance raises the cost of leaving any allowance that can reach it.

These distinctions matter when reading an approval screen in Rabby Wallet or reviewing an approval elsewhere: the dapp’s name, the spender address, and the allowance amount answer different questions. The name helps identify the service; the address identifies the contract with permission; the amount shows the maximum it may move. Disconnecting a wallet from a dapp does not itself change the on-chain allowance. Uniswap Labs explains that revoking costs a network fee and means approving again before the next swap.

Use Uniswap Labs’ explanation of revocation for that distinction, then review the actual allowances by chain and spender. Check the contract’s upgradeability and the amount of that token you keep in the wallet before deciding whether a standing cap is small enough. Review the approvals monthly in revokecash and remove allowances for dapps you have stopped using.

Related

How often should you check token approvals?

Check monthly, and after you stop using a dapp or learn that a spender’s contract has changed. Remove approvals that no longer serve a use.

Is an unlimited approval ever safe?

It can be a convenience choice for a trusted, immutable spender, but it still permits movement up to the full balance of that token. A cap narrows the amount exposed.

Cheapest chain to revoke approvals on?

Fees vary with network conditions, so there is no permanent cheapest chain. Compare the transaction fee shown for the relevant chain before revoking; on a cheap L2, exact approvals may be economical to repeat.

If the dapp is immutable and well known, choose a capped standing approval; if it is upgradeable or holds access to a large token balance, choose exact amounts.
