Fake interview opened path to Binance’s $6.34bn theft

Binance says a fake recruitment process compromised an engineer's device before attackers used the wallet signing service to move $6.34bn in digital assets.

The Hashbeam Desk·Nov 10, 2026 · 00:50 UTC·4 min read

Binance says attackers used a fake job interview to compromise an engineer’s work computer, then reached its wallet signing service and moved about $6.34 billion in digital assets on Oct. 29. The exchange’s initial findings and response update describes a multi-stage intrusion that began months earlier and used Ethereum’s EIP-7702 authorization mechanism to transfer assets outside Binance’s normal withdrawal process.

The fake interview was one stage, not the campaign’s first known foothold. Binance says a separate compromise in June exposed a Wallet DevOps engineer’s role and colleagues after he downloaded a tampered Homebrew installation while looking for an AI coding tool. The exchange detected the anomaly, replaced his device and rotated related credentials, but says attackers retained information about the wallet team.

How did the fake interview reach Binance’s wallet team?

In September, an engineer who listed his Binance experience on a job platform was approached by someone posing as a project director. Binance says the engineer completed two interviews for a fictitious company: one with a supposed HR representative over live video, and another with a purported CEO on a social chat app.

At the second interview, the engineer was told to download a coding assignment to his work computer. Binance says the repository’s code ran during setup, replaced a legitimate development-tool component with a malicious version, and contacted an attacker-controlled server. The exchange described the malware as a zero-day that its endpoint security tools did not detect. It says the attackers also concealed activity and removed traces of the compromise.

On Oct. 20, according to Binance, attackers used that compromised computer and an internal AI tool to gain control of two other engineers’ devices. One held a token granting container deployment permission. On Oct. 29, they used stolen credentials to access another engineer’s computer, deploy a container and run a malicious script against the wallet signing operations.

What did EIP-7702 let the attackers do?

Binance says the attackers used its internal signing service to create EIP-7702 authorizations for a Binance hot wallet. EIP-7702 lets an account delegate specified execution rights to a smart contract without exposing the account’s private key; in this case, the exchange says, an attacker-controlled contract received partial execution rights over the wallet.

Binance says attackers submitted 50 signing transactions to manipulate the account nonce and establish those delegations. Once authorized on-chain, the contract could move assets independently of Binance’s ordinary withdrawal infrastructure. Suspending withdrawals therefore did not invalidate permissions already established on the blockchain, the exchange says.

The incident was not a reported flaw in EIP-7702 itself. The mechanism was used after attackers obtained access to Binance’s signing process. Binance says its hot wallets use multi-party computation (MPC), so no single private key could immediately revoke the authorizations. The exchange had to execute additional transactions to clear them; at 00:10 UTC on Oct. 30, it sent a new authorization from the Binance14 hot wallet to the zero address, clearing its EIP-7702 delegation code.

What is known about the losses and attribution?

Binance’s initial accounting puts affected assets at approximately $6.34 billion. It says about $2.85 billion had been frozen or intercepted as of its Nov. 3 update, with the figure subject to reconciliation and market-price changes. The exchange also said 98% of affected token assets had been replenished as of Oct. 31, and that it would cover the loss rather than pass it to users.

Binance says the FBI attributed the incident to North Korea. CoinDesk’s account of the exchange’s briefing also reports that attribution and describes the intrusion as a months-long campaign. Binance calls its findings preliminary: it says the investigation is ongoing and that independent cybersecurity experts will review the incident.
