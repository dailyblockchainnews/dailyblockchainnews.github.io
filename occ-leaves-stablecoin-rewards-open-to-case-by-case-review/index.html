OCC leaves stablecoin rewards open to case-by-case review

The OCC’s GENIUS Act rule keeps the ban on issuer-paid yield but leaves third-party stablecoin rewards to case-by-case review, without a safe harbor.

The Hashbeam Desk·Oct 22, 2026 · 10:26 UTC·3 min read

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) has finalized its GENIUS Act rule, leaving crypto platforms’ stablecoin rewards subject to case-by-case review rather than a blanket prohibition. The OCC’s announcement of the final rule says the framework takes effect January 18, 2027; its treatment of rewards matters because exchanges can pass value to users even though the law bars stablecoin issuers from paying yield directly to holders.

Which stablecoin payments does the law prohibit?

GENIUS Act section 4(a)(11) bars a permitted payment stablecoin issuer from paying a holder interest or yield “solely in connection with the holding, use, or retention” of the stablecoin. The ban covers payment in cash, tokens or other consideration. That wording makes the reason for a payment central: a reward tied solely to holding the token falls within the prohibition, while the statute does not automatically capture every payment made by a separate platform.

That distinction does not create a categorical exemption for exchanges. The OCC’s rule covers permitted issuers under its jurisdiction, including their relationships with third parties; the agency will apply the statutory language to the facts and circumstances of particular arrangements. In its report on the rule, CoinDesk describes the OCC’s case-by-case approach and notes that the final rule provides no safe harbor for existing rewards programs.

Does an exchange reward qualify automatically?

No. The OCC declined to presume that rewards programs already in operation comply with the law. Their existence before the GENIUS Act does not, by itself, establish that Congress endorsed them. The final rule also removes “rebuttable presumptions” that the OCC had proposed for specified conduct, but that change does not establish that the conduct is allowed. The regulator will assess each arrangement individually.

The word “solely” leaves room for a payment made, at least in part, for another reason to fall outside the ban, CoinDesk reports. But the OCC did not set out examples that would qualify. A platform’s label for a payment—“rewards,” for example—does not settle the question; the arrangement’s facts and purpose matter. The final rule therefore preserves uncertainty for platforms and issuers that share economics or coordinate how rewards reach a stablecoin holder.

What changes when the rule takes effect?

January 18, 2027, is the effective date for the OCC’s final rule and the GENIUS Act, according to the OCC. The rule establishes requirements for stablecoin issuers under OCC jurisdiction and certain custody activities, while other regulators continue work on their parts of the law. It does not newly authorize every exchange reward, nor does it shut them all down: issuer-paid yield remains prohibited under the statutory test, and third-party programs face review under that same test.

The distinction matters because reserve assets backing stablecoins can earn interest, while platforms may offer customers rewards linked to stablecoin balances. The Associated Press reported that banking groups sought tighter restrictions, arguing that higher-yield stablecoin products could draw deposits away from banks. That is an industry concern, not a finding in the OCC rule. For now, the practical compliance question is whether a particular payment is made solely because a customer holds, uses or retains a payment stablecoin—and the OCC has left that judgment to the facts of each program.
