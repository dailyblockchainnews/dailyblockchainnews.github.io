Run your own Ethereum node vs an RPC provider for your workload

Run your own Ethereum node vs use an RPC provider: use a provider for wallets and light apps; run a node when privacy, heavy querying or control matters.

The Hashbeam Desk·Nov 19, 2026 · 17:36 UTC·4 min read

For most wallets and light apps, use an RPC provider; run your own Ethereum node when privacy, heavy querying or control over data matters. A node gives you a direct source of JSON-RPC responses, but it costs hardware, synchronization time and ongoing maintenance.

A node verifies Ethereum data; a provider serves it for you

An Ethereum node combines an execution client with a consensus client, and exposes JSON-RPC through the execution client. A request goes to a method such as reading an account balance; the execution client checks its local chain data and state, then returns a response. With a provider, your app sends that request to the provider’s RPC endpoint, and the provider’s infrastructure returns the response.

Since the Merge in September 2022, an execution client alone no longer follows Ethereum’s proof-of-stake chain. Geth, for example, executes transactions in the Ethereum Virtual Machine and holds execution state, while a paired consensus client tracks the chain’s head and runs proof-of-stake fork choice. The clients communicate over the Engine API: the consensus client supplies execution payloads, and the execution client validates and executes them. Lighthouse is one consensus client; Prysm, Teku, Nimbus and Lodestar are others. Nethermind, Besu, Erigon and Reth are execution alternatives.

That pairing matters even when all you want is a local RPC endpoint: install and configure both clients, then point your wallet or application at the execution client’s JSON-RPC interface. Running only Geth may give the impression of a working local service, but without a consensus client it cannot track Ethereum’s current proof-of-stake chain. You do not need to run a validator just to read data or submit transactions through your node.

A wallet user can choose a public endpoint for an EVM network through Chainlist, an EVM network directory that includes network details such as chain IDs and RPC endpoints. The wallet sends its requests to the selected endpoint. That is a quick way to connect, though the endpoint is operated by someone else.

Choose by privacy, request volume, upkeep and cost

Your workload decides whether the provider’s convenience is worth relying on its limits and data handling. Compare the options against the requests you actually make, how private they need to be, and whether you can maintain a machine that stays synchronized.

  • Privacy: A provider can see your IP address and the queries associated with it. A self-hosted node removes that provider from the request path, although your internet connection and any other services in your app still matter.
  • Request volume: Providers commonly impose rate limits, including on free tiers. Your own node has no provider quota, but its hardware and network still constrain how many requests it can serve.
  • Setup and upkeep: A provider gives you an endpoint without client installation or synchronization. A node requires configuring an execution and consensus client, syncing them, monitoring disk space, and applying client updates.
  • Cost: Free provider tiers can suit modest use; higher or steadier demand may require a paid plan. Self-hosting shifts the cost to hardware, storage, electricity and your time. A full node needs a multi-terabyte SSD in many configurations, with the amount depending on client choice and pruning.

For a bot or indexer making sustained or private queries, a self-hosted node can be the better fit: requests stay under your control, and provider rate limits do not determine the workload. Plan for storage growth and syncing time, and make sure your app handles the node being unavailable during maintenance or recovery. If your workload depends on historical data, confirm that the chosen client and pruning setup retain the data your queries require.

For a wallet user making ordinary balance checks and transaction requests, a public provider endpoint is usually the practical choice. It avoids running two clients and waiting for synchronization. A free tier may be enough, but check its request limits and have a fallback endpoint if interruptions would affect your use.

Use your own node when heavy or private querying makes provider limits or provider visibility unacceptable. Otherwise, use a provider and pick its plan based on expected volume, reliability needs and cost.

Related

How much storage does an Ethereum full node need?

An Ethereum full node needs storage sized for both its execution and consensus clients. The exact space varies with the clients, configuration and pruning strategy, so check their current requirements and leave room for synchronization and database maintenance. An archive node, which retains additional historical state, has different storage needs from a pruned full node.

Can I point MetaMask at my own node?

Yes. MetaMask can use a custom RPC endpoint, so you can enter the endpoint exposed by your own execution client. Make sure the consensus client is paired and synchronized, and configure the endpoint so it is reachable from the device running MetaMask.
