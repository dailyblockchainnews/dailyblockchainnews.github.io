Fairshake targets 32 House races after CLARITY Act stalls

Fairshake named 32 House incumbents for 2026 support, including six slated for $1 million each, as the Senate’s CLARITY Act effort remains stalled.

The Hashbeam Desk·Oct 6, 2026·3 min read

Fairshake said Monday it will support 32 House incumbents in November’s midterms, including six candidates slated for $1 million in spending each, as the crypto industry turns to elections after the Senate blocked its market-structure bill. The Associated Press report says the slate includes 19 Republicans and 13 Democrats; all backed legislation that was a priority for the industry.

The six receiving the largest disclosed amounts are Reps. Janelle Bynum of Oregon, Steven Horsford of Nevada and Derek Tran of California, all Democrats, and Republicans French Hill of Arkansas, Bryan Steil of Wisconsin and Bill Huizenga of Michigan. Fairshake did not disclose its planned spending on the other 26 candidates, according to Roll Call’s report. The announcement describes a slate of supported candidates, but does not give a total budget for the House races.

How will Fairshake spend the money?

Fairshake is a federal independent expenditure-only committee, a type of super PAC. Its own description of its committee says it supports candidates solely through independent activities. That means the announced support takes the form of spending independent of the candidates’ campaigns, rather than contributions paid directly to their campaign committees.

The distinction matters because the $1 million figures describe planned spending, not money handed to the six lawmakers. Nor does the announcement establish how much each of the other 26 candidates will receive in support. Fairshake’s spokesperson Geoff Vetter did not give those amounts. Roll Call reported that Fairshake had $108 million in cash at the end of August, citing Federal Election Commission data. The AP said Fairshake and affiliated PACs together reported about $120 million on hand at that point; those figures cover different sets of committees.

The PAC framed its activity as support for candidates in both parties who favor crypto, not as an effort limited to one party. Vetter said Fairshake had engaged in 57 races during the cycle and won 53. That is the organization’s characterization of its record; the statement does not specify which races count as engaged or define “won.”

What stalled in the Senate?

The bill at issue is the CLARITY Act, a market-structure measure intended to set federal rules for digital-asset markets and divide oversight between regulators. The House passed it in July 2025, but the Senate failed to advance it in September. The AP reported that the Senate vote was 49-50; Republicans needed 60 votes to move forward.

The sticking point included ethics safeguards addressing President Donald Trump’s crypto investments. Democrats argued the bill did not adequately address those interests, while negotiations over stronger restrictions and enforcement did not produce an agreement before the vote. The failed vote blocked the bill’s immediate progress; it did not itself enact a different regulatory framework.

Fairshake’s House slate links the industry’s legislative priority to its midterm strategy: all 32 candidates supported the measure, and the committee says it backs candidates based on their crypto positions. The group has also disclosed a much larger planned investment in the Senate contest in Ohio: nearly $30 million against Democrat Sherrod Brown, who is challenging Republican Sen. Jon Husted. Fairshake has not said whether it will spend more in House or Senate races before November, leaving the eventual scale of its election activity unresolved.
