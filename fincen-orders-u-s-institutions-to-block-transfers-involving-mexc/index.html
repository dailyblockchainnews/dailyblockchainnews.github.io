FinCEN orders U.S. institutions to block transfers involving MEXC

FinCEN has ordered U.S. institutions to block transfers involving MEXC, citing $16.7 billion in alleged illicit flows and a Russian finance finding.

The Hashbeam Desk·Nov 9, 2026 · 11:12 UTC·3 min read

FinCEN has ordered U.S. financial institutions to stop sending funds to or receiving them from crypto exchange MEXC Global, citing $16.7 billion in transactions it links to illicit finance. The order, issued under Section 9714 of the Combating Russian Money Laundering Act, takes effect 30 days after publication in the Federal Register. Treasury announced the action on Nov. 6 as part of Operation Economic Outcast; its announcement says the transfers were connected to sanctions evasion, cybercrime and other illicit activity.

How does the order block transfers involving MEXC?

It bars covered U.S. financial institutions from any transmittal of funds to or from MEXC Global. The FinCEN order defines funds to include convertible virtual currency, so the prohibition covers crypto as well as conventional transfers. It applies to MEXC’s foreign operations, including its subsidiaries, branches, offices and successor entities; U.S.-based affiliates are expressly excluded from the order’s definition of MEXC.

The measure reaches beyond correspondent accounts. FinCEN says that restricting only those accounts would leave other routes open, including indirect dollar access through foreign financial institutions. Under the order, covered institutions must reject certain incoming crypto transfers from MEXC when they can identify and reject them. FinCEN recognizes a limit: crypto networks can make it difficult or impossible for an institution to stop an incoming transfer before receipt, especially when the sending address is outside its control. The order gives institutions discretion to handle such cases based on the circumstances.

Why does FinCEN base the action on Russian illicit finance?

Section 9714 authorizes Treasury to restrict transfers involving a foreign financial institution when it finds the institution to be of primary money-laundering concern in connection with Russian illicit finance. FinCEN made that finding about MEXC. Treasury announced the order under its Iran-focused Operation Economic Outcast, but the statutory finding that supports the transfer ban is tied to Russia.

FinCEN says that between May 2021 and May 2026, MEXC facilitated $16.7 billion in funds connected to Russian illicit finance and other illicit activity. It identifies nearly $1.5 billion in exposure to transactions linked to Russian sanctions evasion, including $232 million in direct transactions with U.S.-, EU- or UK-sanctioned entities. The order also cites continued transactions with the Russian exchanges Garantex and Grinex as recently as June 2026. These are findings and allegations by FinCEN, not a separate adjudication of each transaction.

When does the ban take effect, and what remains allowed?

The order says it becomes effective 30 days after Federal Register publication. The version posted by FinCEN says it had been submitted for publication and was pending public display, so the 30-day period is tied to that publication date. The action does not itself freeze customer balances or prohibit all activity on MEXC; it restricts covered U.S. institutions from transferring funds to or from the defined MEXC entities.

The order also leaves existing Bank Secrecy Act duties, including suspicious activity reporting, and separate sanctions administered by OFAC in place. CoinDesk reported that MEXC said it was operating normally while reviewing the order and its anti-money-laundering and sanctions controls. The practical reach of the measure therefore depends on whether a transfer involves a covered institution and whether crypto transfers can be identified and rejected at the point of receipt.
