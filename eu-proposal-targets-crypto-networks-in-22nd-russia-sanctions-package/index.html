EU proposal targets crypto networks in 22nd Russia sanctions package

The European Commission’s 22nd Russia sanctions proposal would extend transaction bans to 60 banks and target crypto networks, but leaves the crypto measures’ scope unspecified.

The Hashbeam Desk·Nov 24, 2026 · 01:56 UTC·3 min read

The European Commission has proposed targeting Russian crypto platforms and payment networks in its 22nd sanctions package, announced on November 23, alongside transaction bans on about 60 Russian banks. The measures are proposals, not rules in force: the Associated Press report says all 27 EU member countries must endorse them, a process that can take weeks and may change the package.

Commission President Ursula von der Leyen’s statement on the package describes the financial measures at a high level. The Commission would extend the transaction ban to roughly 60 Russian banks and remove those banks from SWIFT, the messaging system used to exchange payment instructions between financial institutions. The statement does not name the banks or set out the proposed legal text, so it does not establish which transactions would be covered or when restrictions would take effect.

For crypto, the Commission says it intends to crack down on crypto and payment networks in Russia and in third countries that help Russia evade sanctions. It also says it has launched a Crypto Sanctions Task Force to support that effort. The statement does not name a cryptoasset, exchange, wallet provider, blockchain, or payment protocol targeted by the proposal. It does not specify whether the measures would prohibit particular transactions, impose asset freezes, or extend existing restrictions to additional providers.

That distinction matters for implementation. A transaction ban on a bank and a restriction aimed at a crypto service provider operate through different intermediaries: the first concerns dealings with a named financial institution, while the Commission’s statement describes the crypto target by its alleged role in sanctions evasion. The announcement supplies no proposed test for identifying a covered third-country network or provider. Until the legal acts are published and adopted, the statement does not define how the crypto restriction would apply to a platform, its customers, or transfers routed through it.

The proposal builds on EU sanctions already aimed at Russia’s financial links, but the announcement does not say whether the new crypto provisions would amend existing rules or create a separate basis for action. Nor does it identify the third countries or platforms under consideration. The task force is presented as an enforcement mechanism to support the work; the Commission’s statement gives no membership, powers, or operating timetable.

The package also proposes listing more than 70 vessels linked to Russia’s shadow fleet and a ban on tanker vessels insured in Russia. On sports and culture, the Commission proposes barring Russian teams and athletes from competing under the Russian flag at events in the EU, prohibiting official Russian symbols, and restricting financial flows from Russian sponsors to European-based sports and cultural associations. These are components of the same package, but the Commission has not said they will enter into force before member states approve the proposals.

For crypto markets, the confirmed development is the Commission’s proposal and the formation of its task force, not a new blanket ban already binding on exchanges or users. The practical scope depends on the measures member states ultimately adopt and the legal text that implements them. The announcement confirms the policy target—networks used to help Russia evade sanctions—but leaves the technical and legal criteria for applying that target open.
