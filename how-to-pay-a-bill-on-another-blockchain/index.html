How to Pay a Bill on Another Blockchain

Paying across chains means matching the invoice’s network and asset, then sending funds there directly or bridging them before making a separate payment.

The Hashbeam Desk·Oct 23, 2026 · 16:56 UTC·4 min read

To pay a bill on another blockchain, send the requested asset on the exact network named by the recipient, or move funds to that network first and pay from there. A wallet address alone is not enough to identify the destination: the same address format can exist on multiple chains, and a transfer on one chain does not appear as a payment on another. The invoice’s network, token and recipient address define the payment.

If you already hold the requested asset on the destination chain, pay directly from a wallet connected to that network. If you do not, a bridge can transfer or recreate value across chains, but bridging and paying are usually separate operations. A portal bridge route is one way to think about that movement; the route’s asset and settlement mechanics determine what arrives. The bridge transfer does not by itself settle the bill unless the payment flow explicitly combines both actions.

What details do I need from the invoice?

Before moving funds, confirm the destination network, token, amount, recipient address and deadline. Treat these as a single payment instruction. A token symbol such as USDC does not uniquely identify an asset: a chain may have the issuer’s native token, a bridged representation, or another token using the same symbol. The recipient’s instructions determine which one counts.

Also check who pays network fees and which asset pays them. A wallet needs the destination chain’s native gas token to submit an ordinary transaction there, even if the bill is denominated in a stablecoin. A bridge may charge a separate fee, deduct an amount from the transfer, or require source-chain gas. The displayed amount to be received should cover the invoice after any route fees and slippage.

Addresses can be misleading across networks. An EVM wallet may show the same hexadecimal address on several EVM chains, but each chain maintains separate state: sending on the wrong chain credits that address on the wrong ledger. Other networks use different address formats and may require extra fields, such as a memo or destination tag. Copy the recipient details from the invoice and verify the network in the wallet before signing.

How do I move funds to the payment network?

To bridge funds, choose a route that supports both the source and destination networks and the specific asset the invoice accepts. The source transaction may lock tokens in a contract, burn them, or send them to a liquidity provider. A bridge then verifies or attests to that source action and makes value available on the destination chain, commonly by releasing liquidity or minting a representation. The exact trust model and asset received depend on the route.

Once funds arrive, make the bill payment as a separate destination-chain transaction: enter the recipient address, token contract or asset, and invoice amount, then review the wallet’s network and transaction summary before signing. If a service offers a combined bridge-and-call flow, it can submit a destination-chain action after the transfer, but only when that route supports the required call and the recipient can accept it. Do not assume a bridge automatically forwards the funds to the payee.

  • Compare the source and destination networks against the invoice.
  • Confirm the exact token accepted, including whether it is native or bridged.
  • Check the estimated received amount, route fee, slippage and required gas tokens.
  • Keep the source transaction hash and check the destination transaction before treating the bill as paid.

What can go wrong after I send?

A bridge transfer and a bill payment have different completion points. The source-chain transaction can be confirmed while the destination transfer is still pending. A route can fail, pause, or deliver less than the invoice amount after fees; a destination transaction can then fail for lack of gas or because the recipient rejects that token. In those cases, the source transaction hash is not proof that the payee received a valid payment.

Use the bridge’s status page or the relevant chain explorers to follow the source and destination transactions. Confirm that the destination transaction succeeded, the correct asset reached the intended wallet, and the recipient’s payment system recognizes it. If the bridge delivered a different representation than the invoice accepts, do not send it as though it were interchangeable; first determine whether the recipient can accept it or whether it must be exchanged on the destination chain.

For most bill payments, the cleaner choice is to acquire the accepted asset on the requested network before the due date and pay directly. That removes the bridge’s extra settlement step and its route-specific trust assumptions. When bridging is necessary, budget for both legs, verify the received asset, and treat the bill as settled only after the recipient-side payment transaction succeeds.
