What Is a Relayer Fee in a Bridge Quote? Gas and Capital

A relayer fee in a bridge quote pays destination gas and the capital advanced for a fast fill; see why the gas share falls as transfer size rises.

The Hashbeam Desk·Nov 28, 2026 · 05:27 UTC·5 min read

A relayer fee pays for the party that delivers funds on the destination chain: destination gas and the cost of advancing capital until repayment. The relayer can fill your transfer from its own balance before the origin deposit settles, so the fee covers both the destination transaction and the time its funds are tied up. For more on routes and quote costs, see lifi bridge.

You meet this line when comparing routes for the same token pair: one quote may deliver funds quickly from a relayer’s inventory, while another route may use a different bridge mechanism and show different fee components. The word “relayer” names the party doing the fill; the fee is the amount charged for that service.

What does the relayer fee pay for?

The relayer fee pays for destination-chain execution and the capital a relayer advances to complete a fast transfer. Those costs move for different reasons, so reading them separately helps explain why a quote changes with chain and amount.

The gas component covers the relayer’s transaction on the destination chain. Its cost depends on the destination network’s gas market and the transaction needed to fill the transfer. For the same route and transaction shape, this is broadly a per-transfer cost: it does not rise in proportion to the amount being bridged. A destination fill on Ethereum mainnet can therefore carry a much larger gas cost than one on a lower-cost destination chain.

The capital component compensates the relayer for using its own funds while it waits to be repaid. A larger transfer ties up more capital, so this part generally grows with the amount. The waiting period and the route’s pricing also matter; the quote reflects the provider’s terms at that moment.

Across documents an LP fee and a relayer fee as separate charges. Its relayer fee covers relayer capital cost and destination gas, while the LP fee compensates liquidity providers for capital use and rebalancing risk. These are different participants and different costs, even though both relate to making liquidity available across chains.

LI.FI API quotes expose fee information through feeCosts and gas estimates through gasCosts. Those fields help inspect a quote, but their contents depend on the route and provider. A route through Across can have a different fee breakdown from a route through Relay; compare the quote’s fee details and estimated output rather than assuming every provider uses the same labels.

Why does the same route cost a different percentage?

For the same route, the gas part is broadly fixed per transfer while the capital part scales with the amount, so a small transfer usually pays a higher fee percentage. The fixed cost is divided across fewer tokens; on a larger transfer, that same gas amount takes a smaller share.

Say two illustrative quotes use the same tokens, route, destination chain, and transaction shape, but one transfer is larger. The gas requirement is similar, while the relayer advances more capital for the larger fill. The total fee rises, but the gas portion is spread over more value. As a result, the fee percentage can fall as transfer size increases.

Now keep the transfer size and route structure the same but change the destination to Ethereum mainnet. The destination gas market can make the gas component jump, raising the quote even though the capital amount is unchanged. Gas prices also move over time, so quotes for the same route and amount can differ when requested at different moments.

In practice, inspect both the fee and the amount you expect to receive. A low displayed percentage can still accompany a different output if the route, destination, or included costs changed. LI.FI’s separate fee and gas fields are useful for that comparison; they do not make every bridge’s fee model identical.

Relayer fee vs LP fee: what is the difference?

The relayer fee pays the party that fills your transfer using its own funds; the LP fee pays liquidity providers under the bridge’s fee model. In Across, the relayer fills on the destination chain and is later repaid from the transfer’s origin-side funds through settlement. Liquidity providers support the pool and its rebalancing.

The visible distinction is the recipient and cost being priced: relayer gas and advanced capital belong to the relayer side, while the LP fee is a separate provider charge. Confusing the two can lead you to treat the LP fee as destination transaction gas, or to compare a total fee in one quote with only one component in another.

The relayer fee is typically reflected in the amount delivered, while the gas you pay to submit a source-chain transaction is a separate wallet cost. Quote formats can show costs in different fields, so check whether a listed item is included in the transfer amount or paid separately. In a LI.FI API response, feeCosts and gasCosts provide categories to inspect; the particular route determines what each item represents.

Is the relayer fee refunded if a transfer fails?

Refund handling depends on the bridge route and where the failure occurs. A failed source transaction, a submitted deposit that is later refunded, and a destination fill that fails are different cases with different status and recovery paths. Check the transfer’s route-specific status and recovery instructions before treating any quoted fee as refundable.

The fee line describes the quoted cost of completing the route; it does not by itself describe the recovery outcome for every failure. For a particular transfer, use its bridge status and the provider’s failure handling to determine whether funds or costs are returned.
