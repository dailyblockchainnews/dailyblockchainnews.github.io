What Counts as Proof of a Cross-Chain Transfer?

A useful cross-chain support ticket pairs the source receipt with the bridge message and destination execution, because one hash rarely proves delivery.

The Hashbeam Desk·Oct 16, 2026 · 17:41 UTC·5 min read

Proof of a cross-chain transfer is a linked record of the source transaction, the bridge’s message or transfer event, and the destination outcome. A source-chain hash shows that a transaction was submitted there; by itself, it does not show that another chain accepted the message or credited the recipient. For support, the useful evidence is the chain of records that lets someone match the debit to the attempted delivery.

Start with the bridge or wallet’s transaction history and record the source network, destination network, token, amount, recipient address, and source transaction hash. If the interface gives a message ID or a destination transaction hash, include those too. A walkthrough of the ParaSwap bridge-and-swap flow can help distinguish the wallet’s signed transaction from the later steps that complete a cross-chain route.

What does a source transaction prove?

A source transaction proves only what happened on its own chain, and its receipt is the first record to inspect. On EVM networks, an explorer or an RPC call to eth_getTransactionReceipt returns fields including the transaction’s status and its emitted logs. A status of 0x1 means execution succeeded; 0x0 means it failed. A pending transaction may have no receipt yet.

Success is not the same as successful bridging. A router call can complete while its logs show a different asset movement than expected, and a transaction can transfer tokens without initiating the bridge action the user intended. Inspect the relevant contract logs, not just the green “Success” label. Depending on the bridge design, the source contract may lock or burn tokens, emit a deposit or message event, or hand the transfer to another contract. The event’s emitting address and decoded fields should fit the expected route, token, amount, recipient, and destination network.

Receipts and logs are chain-specific records. They do not automatically prove to a support agent that the destination chain has accepted a message. The bridge’s own tracker may identify the corresponding message using a protocol-specific ID; when available, that ID is the clearest join between the source event and destination execution. A destination transaction hash is a separate record, not a transformed version of the source hash.

How do you match the transfer on the destination chain?

Match the destination result against the source intent: same recipient, expected destination network, and the expected token or its documented representation. On an EVM destination, inspect the destination transaction receipt and relevant logs as well. A successful receipt establishes successful execution of that destination transaction; the transfer or bridge event establishes what that execution did. If the destination transaction reverted, the receipt can show that failure even though the source transaction succeeded.

Bridge designs differ, so the intermediate record differs too. A lock-and-mint route may lock an asset on one chain and mint a representation on another; a burn-and-release route burns the representation before releasing the original asset. Liquidity-based routes may pay the recipient from destination-side liquidity and settle between providers separately. These models do not share one universal event name or one universal proof format. Use the bridge’s own tracker to locate the relevant message and interpret its states, then verify any claimed destination delivery against the destination chain.

When a destination transaction is not visible yet, the source may still be waiting for the bridge’s confirmation threshold, verification, relayer or executor work, or a required claim step. The exact stages depend on the bridge and the chains involved. “Source confirmed” and “destination delivered” are different states; a tracker’s pending label can describe the interval between them rather than a lost transfer.

What should you send support?

Send enough public, transaction-specific data to reproduce the lookup. A concise ticket should include:

  • Source and destination network names, plus the source transaction hash.
  • The bridge or route name, token and amount sent, and intended recipient address.
  • The bridge message ID, destination transaction hash, or tracker link, if one is available.
  • What the tracker reports and what you see in the destination wallet, including the token contract address if the balance is missing.

Hashes and addresses are public, but a screenshot alone is weaker than explorer records because it may omit the network, contract, or receipt status. If the recipient address is correct and the destination transaction succeeded, check that the wallet is viewing the destination network and the correct token contract. A token balance can be present on-chain without appearing under the wallet’s default token list.

When is the transfer actually proven?

A transfer is supported by evidence when the records connect the intended source action to the bridge’s identified message and the destination effect. One hash can establish a useful step, but it rarely establishes the whole route. If the source receipt shows failure, there was no successful source execution. If it shows success but no matching bridge event, the bridge may not have received the intended instruction. If the bridge reports delivery but no destination effect is visible, compare the destination hash, recipient, and token contract before treating the ticket as resolved.

For most support cases, provide the source hash first, then the bridge’s message ID and destination hash as they become available. That sequence separates confirmed chain records from tracker status or user claims, and makes the point of failure testable.
