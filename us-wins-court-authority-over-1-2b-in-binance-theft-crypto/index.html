US Wins Court Authority Over $1.2B in Binance Theft Crypto

The Justice Department says a court authorized seizure or restraint of over $1.2 billion from more than $2.7 billion frozen after Binance’s October theft.

The Hashbeam Desk·Nov 18, 2026 · 17:45 UTC·3 min read

The US Justice Department has obtained court authorization to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion in cryptocurrency stolen from Binance, while the government and private sector have frozen more than $2.7 billion of the stolen funds. The Associated Press report says the FBI described the action as a seizure; the department’s announcement uses the more specific phrase “seizure and restraint.” The distinction matters: court authorization to seize or restrain assets does not, by itself, establish that all of the authorized cryptocurrency has been transferred to government custody or returned to victims.

Three figures describe different stages

  • More than $6 billion stolen: The FBI says North Korean military members it calls TraderTraitor stole more than $6 billion in virtual assets from Binance on or about October 29. AP describes the theft as about $6.3 billion.
  • More than $2.7 billion frozen: The Justice Department says combined government and private-sector efforts have frozen more than $2.7 billion of the stolen funds. A freeze restricts movement of assets under the control of the party applying it; the announcement does not say that this entire amount has been seized by the government.
  • More than $1.2 billion covered by court authorization: This is a subset of the frozen funds, according to the department. The announcement does not give a breakdown by asset, blockchain, or custodian, or say how much has been transferred into government-controlled wallets.

The department announced the action on November 12. It described the $1.2 billion as cryptocurrency “recently stolen from Binance” and said the authorization allows the government to seize or restrain it. That wording is narrower than saying the US has completed recovery of the assets. AP reports that the government typically files a forfeiture claim before a judge, who then decides who has a legitimate claim to recovered funds. The department’s announcement does not identify the court or say that a forfeiture judgment has been entered.

TraderTraitor’s laundering path remains in motion

The FBI’s October 31 advisory attributed the Binance theft to North Korea and named the activity TraderTraitor. The FBI alert says investigators observed the actors converting stolen assets primarily into Ether (ETH). The bureau expected further laundering through decentralized finance services and instant-swap services, and urged operators in those services, along with node operators and other virtual-asset providers, to block transactions with or derived from addresses the actors were using.

That advisory describes the operational response as address-based: the FBI published addresses it said were holding or had held assets from the theft, and asked service operators to block related transactions. It does not say that every listed address or asset can be frozen by a court order. The Justice Department’s November announcement likewise does not identify which assets or addresses are covered by the more than $1.2 billion authorization. The public figures therefore show the scale of the coordinated freeze and the legal authority secured over part of it, but not a complete accounting of where every stolen asset sits.

The recovery process also has a claims stage. AP says a judge would determine who has a legitimate claim to recovered funds after the government files for forfeiture. Until that process is resolved, the announced authorization should not be read as proof that Binance customers have received the stolen cryptocurrency back. The confirmed development is a legal step over more than $1.2 billion within a larger pool of funds that authorities and private companies say they have frozen.
