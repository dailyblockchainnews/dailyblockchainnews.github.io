How do common crypto bridge scams trick people into signing?

Common crypto bridge scams and how they work: fake sites, malicious approvals, recovery DMs and poisoned addresses; learn the tell and the safe habit for each.

The Hashbeam Desk·Nov 28, 2026 · 05:49 UTC·4 min read

Common crypto bridge scams trick people into signing malicious approvals, revealing wallet secrets or sending tokens to a scammer’s address.

Imagine a friend trying to move tokens to another chain. A search ad leads them to a convincing copy of a bridge site. The site requests an approval, and the wallet displays a contract address and spending permission. If your friend signs without checking what the approval authorizes, the scam contract may be able to move the approved tokens.

How do common crypto bridge scams get people to give up control?

Fake bridge sites use a copied interface to make a malicious approval look like part of a normal transfer. The attacker may buy an ad or share a link in a chat, then ask visitors to connect a wallet and approve a contract. The approval is the key step: it grants a spender permission to use tokens, so a later transaction from that spender can move them.

To check whether a bridge website is real, start from a domain you already trust or find through an independent, reliable source. Don’t rely on an ad, a message or a search result that could point to a copy. Before signing, read the wallet request: identify the contract receiving permission and whether the request authorizes token spending. A familiar-looking page does not establish that its contract is legitimate.

A separate trap is a “free” signature request that looks harmless because it asks for no gas. An EIP-2612 permit is signed off-chain, so signing it costs no gas; it can still authorize a spender to use tokens. A malicious spender can submit the permit and then call a token transfer function to take the authorized amount. The absence of a gas charge says nothing about whether the permission is safe.

LI.FI is a cross-chain bridge aggregator, so its service helps people find routes for moving crypto between blockchains. A genuine delay does not require handing control to someone who contacts you. For a delayed lifi bridge transfer, use the source transaction hash to track it yourself in LI.FI Explorer.

Fake support accounts exploit the same anxiety. Someone in Discord or Telegram may message about a “stuck transfer,” ask for a seed phrase, or send a recovery link that leads to a malicious site. A seed phrase gives control of the wallet, and no legitimate wallet or bridge support needs it. Don’t reply with it or enter it into a link sent in a DM.

What should you do after a bad approval or suspicious transfer?

After signing a suspicious token approval, revoke the allowance promptly and move any remaining funds to a fresh wallet. Revoking removes the spender’s permission for future token transfers under that allowance; it cannot reverse transfers already completed. Use a reputable allowance checker such as Revoke.cash or Etherscan, and make sure you are interacting with the intended service before signing a revocation.

If the seed phrase may have been exposed, treat the wallet as compromised: revoking one allowance is not enough, because the phrase can give an attacker broader control. Create a fresh wallet with a new seed phrase and transfer remaining assets there. Never enter the old phrase into a “recovery” page, even if the person contacting you claims to be support.

Address poisoning targets the moment someone copies a recipient from wallet history. The scammer sends a zero-value or dust transfer from a vanity address whose first and last characters match those of a real address. That transfer places the look-alike in the victim’s own activity history. Later, the victim may copy the wrong entry and send funds to the scammer. Compare the full destination address and copy it from a saved contact; never take it from recent activity.

Related

How to check if a bridge website is real?

Open the site through a trusted source, then inspect the wallet request before signing. Check which contract receives permission and what the permission allows. A copied interface or a plausible name is not proof that the contract belongs to the service you meant to use.

What to do after signing a malicious approval?

Revoke the allowance using Revoke.cash or Etherscan, then move remaining funds to a fresh wallet. A revocation blocks later use of that permission; it does not recover tokens already moved.

Are DMs offering to recover stuck bridge funds a scam?

Treat unsolicited recovery DMs as a scam attempt. Don’t share a seed phrase or open their recovery link. Track a delayed transfer yourself with its source hash in LI.FI Explorer.
