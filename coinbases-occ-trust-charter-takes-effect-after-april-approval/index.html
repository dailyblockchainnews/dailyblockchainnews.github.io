Coinbase’s OCC trust charter takes effect after April approval

Coinbase National Trust Company became effective November 6, moving its custody business into an OCC framework while remaining a non-insured trust bank.

The Hashbeam Desk·Nov 11, 2026 · 10:18 UTC·3 min read

Coinbase National Trust Company’s national trust charter became effective November 6, moving the company’s proposed custody operations under the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC), the federal banking regulator. The OCC’s application record shows two distinct milestones: conditional approval on April 2, then consummation and effectiveness on November 6. The OCC’s corporate application record confirms the effective date and classifies the institution as a national trust bank.

Coinbase announced the final approval on November 9. The charter creates a federally chartered trust company for custody activity; it does not by itself make Coinbase a full-service commercial bank. The company’s announcement of Coinbase National Trust Company describes the change as bringing its custody capabilities into a federal regulatory framework.

What changed between April and November?

The OCC approved the charter application in April, but the bank did not become effective at that point. The regulator’s record marks “Approved” on April 2 and “Consummated/Effective” on November 6. That distinction matters: conditional approval lets organizers complete the work required to establish the institution, while consummation records that the charter has taken effect.

Coinbase filed to organize Coinbase National Trust Company as a non-insured national trust company. The new entity is designed around fiduciary services, including digital-asset custody, rather than insured retail deposits. The final step places that trust activity under the OCC’s federal supervisory framework. Coinbase said this will provide a consistent federal framework for its custody business while building on its existing relationship with New York’s Department of Financial Services, which approved a limited-purpose trust charter for Coinbase Custody Trust Company in 2018.

What custody work is the charter for?

The application describes Coinbase National Trust Company as an expansion of Coinbase’s institutional custody business. In custody, the company holds client assets for safekeeping under a fiduciary relationship; the assets remain in custody while clients use related services. Coinbase’s filing described two custody products: Prime Vault, which stores digital assets in segregated arrangements with customer-specific onchain wallet addresses, and Prime Custody, which uses an omnibus wallet for clients trading while assets remain within the qualified-custodian environment. Those are descriptions in the application, not confirmation that every product has moved to the new entity.

The filing also described operational links that would let custody clients access staking, financing and trading services offered by other Coinbase entities. That structure separates custody from the other services: Coinbase National Trust Company would provide the fiduciary custody layer, while affiliated entities provide connected products. The charter’s practical effect is to place that custody layer under federal banking supervision; it does not mean the OCC has approved each connected product as a function of the trust company.

Does the charter make Coinbase a deposit-taking bank?

No. The application sought a non-insured national trust company charter, and Coinbase said the charter concerns custody rather than full-service banking. It does not establish that Coinbase National Trust Company will take retail deposits, offer checking or savings accounts, or make ordinary commercial loans. The OCC record identifies the institution specifically as a trust bank, a charter category whose scope is tied to trust powers.

For institutional clients, the regulatory change is the federal supervision of the trust entity holding custody assets. Coinbase said it will begin with its core custodial offering and described the charter as a basis for expanding that business. The OCC record confirms the charter is effective; it does not specify customer migration dates or establish that existing custody accounts have already transferred to the new institution.
